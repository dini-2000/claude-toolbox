# Summary {-}

Easton ran a shared e-bike pilot from April to September 2026, with 300 bikes at 40 docks in the city centre and the two university campuses.

::: keyfigs
- **61,800** trips in six months
- **4,950** people rode at least once
- **3.1 km** median trip length
- **$1.92** cost to the city per trip
:::

Use grew every month until the August holidays. One trip in three replaced a car or taxi journey, by riders' own account. The bikes cost the city $118,500 after fares, within the $125,000 budget.

The Lab suggests a two-year scheme of 600 bikes, with docks added in the three districts the pilot did not reach.

# The pilot

The city leased 300 e-bikes and 40 docks from an operator for six months. Riders paid $1 to unlock a bike and 15 cents a minute, with a monthly pass at $25. Students and holders of the city's low-income card paid half.

Docks sat in the city centre, at both university campuses and at the main railway station^[The station dock opened three weeks late, on 24 April, after a delay with the electricity supply.]. The Lab chose places within 400 metres of each other, so that a rider who found one dock full could walk to the next.

## What we measured

The operator shared anonymous trip records: start and end dock, time and length. The Lab added two surveys of riders, in June and September, with 1,240 replies in all^[Survey replies were weighted by age and home district to match the riders in the trip records.].

# Who rode, and when

## Trips by week

Trips rose from about 1,300 a week in April to a peak of 3,400 in the last week of June, when the two universities finished their exams. They dipped in August and recovered in September.


Weekday trips peaked between 8 and 9 a.m. and between 5 and 6 p.m. Weekend trips were longer and spread across the day.

## Riders

::: cols-2
Students made 44% of trips, a share close to their share of the people who live in the centre. Riders over 55 made 6% of trips, fewer than the Lab expected.

Women made 38% of trips, up from 31% in April. In the September survey, women were more likely than men to name safer junctions as the change that would make them ride more.
:::

## What the trips replaced

Riders were asked how they would have made their last trip without a bike.

Table: How riders would otherwise have travelled, September survey

| Instead of the bike | Share of trips |
|---|---:|
| Walking | 34% |
| Bus or tram | 27% |
| Car, as driver or passenger | 22% |
| Taxi or ride-hailing | 9% |
| Own bike | 5% |
| Would not have travelled | 3% |

# Costs

The pilot cost $236,400, and fares brought in $117,900. The city paid the difference of $118,500, or $1.92 a trip.

Table: Pilot costs and fares, April to September 2026

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Bike and dock lease | $168,000 |
| Charging and repairs | $41,200 |
| Docks, signs and electricity | $19,600 |
| Surveys and evaluation | $7,600 |
| Fares paid by riders | &minus;$117,900 |
| **Cost to the city** | **$118,500** |

> [!NOTE] Costs per trip fall as use grows
> Most costs are fixed for each bike. The cost to the city per trip fell from $5.10 in April to $1.35 in September^[September had about 12,000 trips. A scheme of 600 bikes would carry the same fixed costs per bike over more trips at each dock.].

# Next steps

The Lab suggests the committee consider three steps for 2027.

::: steps
1. **A two-year scheme.** 600 bikes and 80 docks, tendered in December for a start in April 2027.
2. **New districts.** Docks in Westfield, Old Town and the hospital site, which riders asked for most often.
3. **Safer junctions.** Painted cycle lanes at the six junctions where riders reported the most near misses.
:::

The Lab will publish the full trip data, without personal details, alongside this paper.
